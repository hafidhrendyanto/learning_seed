## My Recap

Economics rests on two core ideas: **scarcity** and **cost**. We have unlimited wants but limited resources, so we're always forced to choose. And every choice — even a seemingly free one — carries a cost. That cost is called **opportunity cost**: the value of the best thing you gave up by choosing something else.

From there, the video builds outward. Because everything has a cost, every decision involves a **trade-off** — and because trade-offs exist, the **incentives** we use to guide decisions matter enormously. Get them wrong, and people make choices that look rational on the surface but harm everyone in the long run. Get them right, and you nudge behavior toward better outcomes.

Finally, economics itself splits into two lenses: **macroeconomics** (the whole economy) and **microeconomics** (individual players). Same principles, different scale.

---

## Key Ideas

### 1. Scarcity — the starting point

Humans want more than the world can provide. That gap — unlimited wants, limited resources — is called scarcity, and it's the reason economics exists at all. If everything were infinitely available, there'd be no need to choose, and therefore no trade-offs, no costs, no economics.

### 2. Opportunity cost — nothing is free

Every choice has a cost: not necessarily money, but the next best thing you gave up. Writing these notes feels free, but the opportunity cost is whatever else you could've done with that time. This applies to everything — money, time, attention, energy.

### 3. Trade-offs — benefits vs. costs

Because everything has a cost, every decision is a trade-off. The real question is never "is there a cost?" — there always is. The question is: **is the benefit worth it?**

- **Cars**: Thousands die in accidents every year. Banning cars would eliminate that — but the loss of mobility and productivity would far outweigh the lives saved. Society accepts the trade-off.
- **Military spending**: The US spends ~$600B/year on defense. That's $600B unavailable for healthcare, education, or infrastructure. There's no neutral choice — every dollar spent is a dollar away from something else.

President Eisenhower put it starkly in 1953, during the Cold War arms buildup:

> *"Every gun that is made, every warship launched, every rocket fired signifies, in the final sense, a theft from those who hunger and are not fed, those who are cold and are not clothed. This world in arms is not spending money alone. It is spending the sweat of its laborers, the genius of its scientists, the hopes of its children."*
> — Dwight D. Eisenhower, 1953

### 4. Incentives — what drives the choice

Given that resources are scarce and every decision has a cost, *how* decisions actually get made comes down to incentives. The right incentives push people toward better choices; the wrong ones distort behavior even when individuals are acting rationally.

- **Bad incentive**: Universities funded per enrolled student → focus shifted to recruitment over student success
- **Better incentive**: Fund based on graduation rates → universities are now motivated to actually help students finish
- The same logic applies at the government level — good policy requires both good data and well-designed incentives

*The video's link between trade-offs and incentives was vague, but the thread is: scarcity forces choices, trade-offs reveal their cost, and incentives are the mechanism that determines which choices actually get made.*

### 5. Macro vs. Micro — two scales of the same lens

- **Macroeconomics** — the economy as a whole: inflation, unemployment, GDP, national policy
- **Microeconomics** — individual players: how a firm decides how many workers to hire, what price to set, whether to expand

Macro asks: *how is the whole forest doing?*  
Micro asks: *how should this one tree grow?*
