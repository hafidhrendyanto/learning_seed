# Secondary Market

The secondary market is where existing securities are traded between investors.

## Key Characteristics

- Investors buy and sell securities **among themselves**
- The original issuer (corporation or government) **does not receive any money**
- Provides [[Liquidity|liquidity]] — the ability to convert securities into cash

## Examples

- Buying Apple (AAPL) stock on the NYSE or NASDAQ
- Selling a government bond you bought last year to another investor
- Trading ETFs through a brokerage app like Robinhood or Fidelity

## Why It Matters

Even though issuers don't get new funds, the secondary market is crucial because:
1. It provides **liquidity** to investors
2. It makes investors more willing to buy in the [[Primary Market|primary market]]
3. It allows portfolio adjustments and price discovery

## Why Secondary Market Prices Matter to Issuers

Even though the issuer receives **no cash** from secondary trades, the **market price** is extremely valuable to the company:

- **Cheaper future capital** — A high stock price means the company can raise more money per share in a future [[Primary Market|primary market]] offering
- **Better collateral** — A high stock price makes it easier to secure loans
- **Employee compensation** — Stock options and equity grants are more attractive and retentive when the stock price is high
- **Acquisition currency** — Companies can buy other companies using shares instead of cash; a high price means fewer shares needed
- **Signaling** — A strong secondary market price signals health and attracts investors
