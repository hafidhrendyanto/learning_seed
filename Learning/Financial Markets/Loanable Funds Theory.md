# Loanable Funds Theory

The loanable funds theory explains how interest rates are determined by the **supply and demand for loanable funds** in financial markets. It connects directly to the concept of [[Surplus and Deficit Units]] — surplus units supply funds, while deficit units demand them.

## Core Idea

Interest rates are the "price" of borrowing money. Like any market, the price is determined where supply equals demand.

## Demand for Loanable Funds

Borrowers who need capital create demand. The aggregate demand is:

$$
D_A = D_h + D_b + D_g + D_m + D_f
$$

Where:
- `D_h` = household demand (housing, autos, consumer spending)
- `D_b` = business demand (long-term investments, projects)
- `D_g` = federal government demand (budget deficits)
- `D_m` = municipal government demand
- `D_f` = foreign demand (affected by domestic vs. U.S. interest rate differentials)

**Key property**: Demand is **inversely related** to interest rates. When rates are low, more projects become profitable and borrowing increases.

## Supply of Loanable Funds

Savers and lenders provide supply. The aggregate supply is:

$$
S_A = S_h + S_b + S_g + S_m + S_f
$$

Where:
- `S_h` = household savings (primary source)
- `S_b` = business cash flows
- `S_g` = federal government revenues
- `S_m` = municipal government revenues
- `S_f` = foreign investment in domestic securities

**Key property**: Supply is **positively related** to interest rates. Higher rates incentivize more saving.

## Equilibrium Interest Rate

The equilibrium occurs where aggregate demand equals aggregate supply:

$$
D_A = S_A
$$

Graphically, this is the intersection of the demand and supply curves.

- **Demand increases** → shortage of funds → rates rise
- **Supply increases** → surplus of funds → rates fall

## Impact of Economic Conditions

### Economic Growth
- Favorable conditions increase business cash flows and investment opportunities
- Demand curve shifts **outward** → **higher equilibrium rates**

### Economic Slowdown
- Fewer profitable projects, reduced borrowing needs
- Demand curve shifts **inward** → **lower equilibrium rates**

## Impact of Inflation

Higher expected inflation:
- Reduces savings (supply shifts inward)
- Increases borrowing (demand shifts outward)
- Result: **higher equilibrium interest rates**

## Fisher Effect

The Fisher effect decomposes the nominal interest rate into:

$$
i = E(INF) + i_R
$$

Where:
- `i` = nominal interest rate
- `E(INF)` = expected inflation rate
- `i_R` = real interest rate

Rearranged:

$$
i_R = i - E(INF)
$$

The real interest rate measures the return earned after adjusting for expected loss in purchasing power.

## Inflation Feedback Loops and Central Bank Role

A critical insight from session discussion: inflation and interest rates can create a **feedback loop** — rising inflation pushes rates up, which can further affect economic behavior. However, this loop is not self-perpetuating indefinitely. Central banks like **the Fed** actively intervene by targeting the **federal funds rate** (the overnight interbank lending rate) to cool or stimulate the economy, breaking runaway feedback.
