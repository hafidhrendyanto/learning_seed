# Depository Institutions

Depository institutions accept **deposits** from the public and use those funds to make loans and purchase securities. They are the most traditional form of financial intermediary.

## Core Advantages

- **Deposit Flexibility** — Offer accounts with various liquidity options (checking, savings, CDs)
- **Fund Repackaging** — Convert many small deposits into larger loans
- **Risk Acceptance** — Bear default risk on behalf of depositors
- **Credit Evaluation** — Specialize in assessing borrower creditworthiness
- **Diversification** — Spread risk across thousands of loans

## Types

### Commercial Banks
The most dominant depository institution. Serve households, businesses, and government agencies.
- Offer wide variety of deposit accounts
- Provide direct loans and purchase debt securities
- Subject to default risk on loans and investments

### Savings Institutions
- Savings & Loan Associations (S&Ls)
- Savings Banks
- Focus mainly on residential mortgages
- Can be shareholder-owned or mutual

### Credit Unions
- Nonprofit depository institutions
- Serve members who share a common bond
- Tend to be smaller due to membership restrictions
- Mainly lend to members

## Deposit Insurance

A critical feature of depository institutions is **government-backed deposit insurance**:
- **United States**: FDIC insures up to $250,000 per depositor
- **Indonesia**: LPS (Lembaga Penjamin Simpanan) provides coverage
- **Purpose**: Protects depositors from losses even if the bank fails

### Why It Matters
- Deposit insurance breaks the direct link between bank loan performance and depositor losses
- Explains why depositors accept lower returns: the government absorbs tail risk
- Without deposit insurance, bank runs would be far more common
- Creates moral hazard: banks might take more risk knowing depositors are protected

## Depository vs. Nondepository: Who Bears the Risk?

Both banks and mutual funds diversify across many loans or securities. The critical difference is **who bears the default risk**:
- **Banks** bear default risk on behalf of depositors. Depositors are shielded (returns are capped, but principal is protected up to insurance limits).
- **Mutual funds** pass default risk directly to investors. Investors own the underlying portfolio and capture the full upside and downside.

This is why banks can offer insured, liquid deposits with lower returns, while mutual funds offer higher return potential with no protection.
