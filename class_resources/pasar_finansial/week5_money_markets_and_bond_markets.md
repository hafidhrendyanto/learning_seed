## 01 Money Market Securities

## Money Market Securities

### Treasury Bills (T-bills)

Short-term securities issued by the U.S. Treasury to finance the budget deficit. They are backed by the federal government, making them virtually free of credit risk and highly liquid.

### Commercial Paper

Short-term debt instruments issued by well-known, creditworthy firms to provide liquidity or finance investments in inventory and accounts receivable. They are typically unsecured and offer a slightly higher yield than T-bills due to credit risk.

## More Money Market Instruments

### Negotiable Certificates of Deposit (NCDs)

Certificates issued by large commercial banks as a short-term source of funds. NCDs have a minimum denomination of $100,000 and maturities ranging from two weeks to one year.

### Repurchase Agreements (Repos)

Agreements where one party sells securities to another with an agreement to repurchase them at a specified date and price. Repos are essentially loans backed by securities.

## Federal Funds and Banker's Acceptances

### Federal Funds

Enable depository institutions to lend or borrow short-term funds from each other at the federal funds rate, influenced by the supply and demand for funds.

### Banker's Acceptances

Indicate that a bank accepts responsibility for a future payment, commonly used for international trade transactions to guarantee payment to exporters.

## 1. Understanding Bond Markets

Bonds are long-term debt securities issued by government agencies or corporations. Most bonds mature in 10–30 years. Bond issuers must pay interest (or coupon) payments annually or semiannually and the par value (principal) at maturity. An issuer must demonstrate that its future cash flows will cover bondholder coupon and principal payments. Investors will only buy questionable-repayment bonds if the expected return outweighs the risk.

Bonds are often categorized by issuer. The U.S. Treasury issues Treasury bonds, federal agencies issue federal agency bonds, state and local governments issue municipal bonds, and corporations issue corporate bonds. A communications network issues primary market bonds. Registered bonds require the issuer to keep track of who owns them so coupon payments can be sent automatically. Financial institutions pay coupons by debiting the issuer's account and crediting the bond owner's account.

Exhibit 7.1 shows how bond markets move money. United States Treasury bonds fund deficit spending on government programs. Federal agencies buy mortgages from financial institutions using bond proceeds. Thus, they indirectly finance home purchases. Companies use bond proceeds to expand. Bond markets boost economic growth by allowing the U.S. government, federal agencies, and corporations to spend more.

## 1 b. Bond Yields

Bond yields reflect anticipated return, influenced by coupon rate, market price, and maturity. Yield to maturity (YTM) shows total return if held to maturity, considering market price, face value, coupon rate, and time to maturity. Premium bonds have lower YTM than coupon, discount bonds have higher YTM.

- Bond yields are a critical metric for investors, reflecting the anticipated return on a bond investment. These yields are influenced by several factors, including the bond's stated coupon rate, its current market price, and the time remaining until its maturity date. Bond yields are not static; they fluctuate daily based on market conditions and vary across different maturities, providing insight into market expectations for future interest rate movements. For example, Indonesian Government bonds (Surat Utang Negara or SUN) yields are closely watched as indicators of Indonesia's economic stability.

- The yield to maturity (YTM) offers a comprehensive view of the total return an investor can expect if the bond is held until maturity. Expressed as an annualized rate, YTM takes into account the bond's current market price, its face value (par value), the coupon interest rate, and the remaining time to maturity. In Indonesia, the YTM for corporate bonds can be significantly influenced by the credit rating of the issuing company and prevailing market sentiment.

- For example, a bond trading at a premium will have a YTM lower than its coupon rate, while a bond trading at a discount will have a higher YTM. In the Indonesian market, if Bank Indonesia (BI) is expected to cut interest rates, bonds, including SUN, may trade at a premium, lowering the YTM relative to the coupon rate. Conversely, uncertainty regarding currency stability can cause bonds to trade at a discount, increasing the YTM.

## 2. Treasury and Federal Agency Bonds

The U.S. government, like many other national governments, commonly spends more money than it receives from taxes. Under these conditions, it needs to borrow funds to cover the difference between what it wants to spend versus what it receives.

To facilitate its fiscal policy, the U.S. Treasury issues Treasury notes and Treasury bonds to finance federal government expenditures.

The Treasury pays a yield to investors that reflects the risk-free rate, as it is presumed that the Treasury will not default on its payments.

Treasury notes and bonds are free from credit (default) risk; they enable the Treasury to borrow funds at a relatively low cost. However, additional borrowing by the U.S. government might potentially cause investors to worry about the Treasury’s ability to cover its debt payments. The minimum denomination for Treasury notes and bonds is now $100.

The key difference between a note and a bond is that note maturities are less than 10 years whereas bond maturities are 10 years or more.

## 2. Treasury and Federal Agency Bonds

### Stripped Treasury Bonds (STRIPS)

STRIPS break Treasury bonds into individual zero-coupon securities, offering flexibility and predictable returns for savings plans.

- STRIPS, or Separate Trading of Registered Interest and Principal Securities, divide a Treasury bond's coupon payments and principal into separate zero-coupon securities. A 10-year Treasury note can become 21 components: 20 coupon payments and a principal payment. Although direct stripping isn't common for Indonesian government bonds (Surat Utang Negara, SUN), similar concepts exist.

- Each component is sold separately for targeted savings or pension fund management. Investors can buy specific coupon payment dates or the final principal repayment. Zero-coupon bonds in Indonesia serve similar purposes, especially in sharia-compliant investments.

- These zero-coupon securities can be traded before maturity, providing flexibility. Returns come from the difference between the purchase price and face value at maturity, attracting investors who want predictable returns without reinvestment risk. In Indonesia, investors can selectively buy bonds with specific maturity dates to align with financial goals, influenced by Bank Indonesia's interest rate policies.

## 2. Treasury and Federal Agency Bonds

### Inflation-Indexed Treasury Bonds (TIPS)

TIPS protect investors from inflation by adjusting the bond's principal based on the Consumer Price Index (CPI) and paying interest on the adjusted principal, ensuring a return above inflation.

- **Principal Adjustment Example (Indonesia):** In Indonesia, inflation-indexed bonds adjust the principal based on the Indonesian CPI. For example, if you invest IDR 10,000,000 in an Indonesian TIPS bond and the CPI rises by 3%, the principal is adjusted to IDR 10,300,000.

- **Coupon Payments Example (Indonesia):** Indonesian TIPS pay interest based on a fixed rate applied to the inflation-adjusted principal. If the TIPS bond has a 2% fixed rate, the interest payment on the adjusted principal (IDR 10,300,000) would be IDR 206,000, ensuring a return above inflation.

## Tax Advantages of Municipal Bonds in Indonesia

Certain Indonesian government bonds (SUN) offer tax-exempt interest, particularly attractive in higher income brackets. Calculate the tax-equivalent yield to compare effectively with taxable investments.

## 4. Corporate Bonds: Funding Business Growth

Corporate bonds are issued by companies to fund growth, offering potentially higher interest rates compared to government bonds. Companies, including Indonesian firms, issue corporate bonds to fund operations, acquisitions, or R&D.

Corporate bonds can be placed with investors through a public offering or a private placement.

### Timing

The yield that corporations pay to investors on the bonds is highly dependent on the market interest rates at the time of the placement. Some corporations attempt to time their bond offerings when market interest rates are low, so that their cost of financing with bonds will be low.

### Private Placement

Some corporate bonds are privately placed rather than sold in a public offering.

### Credit Risk of Corporate Bonds

Corporate bonds are subject to the risk of default, and the yield paid by corporations that issue bonds contains a risk premium to reflect the credit risk. The general level of defaults on corporate bonds is a function of economic conditions.

### Bond Ratings as a Measure of Credit Risk

When corporations issue bonds, they FINANCIAL REFORM hire rating agencies to rate their bonds.

Corporate bonds that receive higher ratings can be placed at higher prices (lower yields) because they are perceived to have lower credit risk.

## Callable Bonds: An Advantage for Issuers

### Advantage for Issuers

Callable bonds allow issuers to redeem bonds early if interest rates fall, reducing borrowing costs. For example, MegaCorp issues callable bonds at 6%; rates fall to 4%. MegaCorp issues new bonds at 4% to retire the 6% bonds. This lowers MegaCorp's borrowing costs, improving its finances. In the US, many corporations used callable bonds in the 1980s to refinance debt at lower rates during periods of economic expansion.

### Risk for Investors

Investors face the risk of early redemption and reinvesting at potentially lower rates. Indonesian bondholders face the same risk when companies call back bonds early due to favorable market conditions. Similarly, US investors in callable bonds must consider this reinvestment risk, particularly when interest rates are volatile.

Callable bonds allow issuers to redeem bonds before maturity at a set price. Consider a similar scenario with Indonesian state-owned enterprises (BUMN) issuing callable bonds. This also applies to Indonesian companies looking to optimize their debt.

## Using Bonds to Finance a Leveraged Buyout

Bonds are crucial for leveraged buyouts (LBOs), where acquired firms' assets secure high-yield bonds. Increased debt can cause distress, but successful LBOs can yield high returns from efficiency, realignment, or sale at a higher valuation.

- **Key Tool in LBOs:** Bonds are a key tool in leveraged buyouts (LBOs), where a company is acquired primarily with borrowed funds.
- **High-Yield Bonds:** Typically, the acquiring firm issues high-yield bonds to finance the majority of the purchase price. For example, a private equity firm might use bond financing to acquire an Indonesian telecom company.
- **Collateral:** The acquired company's assets and cash flows serve as collateral for the debt. For instance, assets of a manufacturing company in Indonesia could be used.
- **Increased Debt Burden Risks:** This increased debt burden can pose significant risks, potentially leading to financial distress if the company fails to meet its debt obligations. This is a risk for Indonesian companies as well.
- **Potential Returns:** However, successful LBOs can generate substantial returns for investors, who benefit from increased efficiency, strategic realignment, or eventual sale of the company at a higher valuation.
- **Scenario:** Consider a scenario where an LBO increases an Indonesian company's value by 20% within five years, allowing the private equity firm to sell it for a significant profit after repaying the bonds.

## Collateralized Debt Obligations (CDOs)

CDOs are complex investments bundling debts into tranches of varying risk. Popular from 2004-2007, they faced criticism for opacity and their role in the 2008 financial crisis due to subprime mortgage reliance and inflated ratings.

- Collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) are complex investments that bundle together various types of debt, such as residential mortgages, commercial loans, and corporate bonds. Indonesian CDOs might include sovereign bonds issued by the Indonesian government or corporate bonds from Indonesian companies.
- These debts are then categorized into tranches based on their risk profile: senior tranches (rated AAA), mezzanine tranches (rated BBB), and equity tranches (unrated). For example, a senior tranche might consist of Indonesian government bonds, considered relatively safe.
- CDOs gained traction between 2004 and 2007 as investors sought higher yields in a low-interest-rate environment. In Indonesia, this period saw increased issuance of CDOs backed by a mix of local and international debt.
- CDOs were also criticized for their opacity and complexity, contributing significantly to the 2008 financial crisis. The lack of transparency in CDOs containing Indonesian assets also posed challenges for investors.
- The over reliance on subprime mortgages within CDOs led to widespread defaults when the housing bubble burst. While Indonesian mortgages were not directly involved, the global impact affected Indonesian markets as well.
- Credit rating agencies like Moody's and Standard & Poor's faced scrutiny for assigning inflated ratings to CDOs, misleading investors about their true risk. Similar concerns arose regarding the ratings of CDOs with exposure to Indonesian debt.

## 09 Other Types of Long-term Debt Securities

## Structured Notes: High Returns, Hidden Risks

Structured notes are debt securities where interest and principal payments depend on specified market conditions—tied to Treasury bond indices, stock indices, or currencies. While they became popular in the 1990s for their high return potential, they carry substantial risks that aren't always apparent.

The most dramatic example: Orange County's 1994 bankruptcy. The portfolio manager invested over $7 billion in structured notes betting interest rates would decline. When rates rose instead, values plummeted. The manager borrowed more to invest further, compounding losses and leading to bankruptcy.

In 2018, Wells Fargo paid $4.1 million to settle SEC charges for failing to disclose structured note risks and fees. Even AAA-rated structured notes proved risky—credit ratings have been frequently revised downward.

### What They Are

Debt securities with payments tied to market conditions like bond prices, stock indices, or currencies

### The Appeal

High return potential and ability to bet indirectly on restricted markets

### The Danger

Complex risk assessment, misleading credit ratings, and potential for catastrophic losses

## Alternative Debt Securities: ETNs and Auction-Rate Securities

### Exchange-Traded Notes (ETNs)

ETNs are debt instruments promising returns based on a specific debt index performance after fees. With maturities of 10 to 30 years, they're issued by firms like Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley but aren't secured by assets, creating default risk.

Because ETNs can contain commodities and foreign currencies, they avoid mutual fund regulations and can use leverage—borrowed funds that magnify both gains and losses. This creates higher potential returns but significantly greater risk.

### Auction-Rate Securities

Used since the 1980s by municipalities and student loan organizations, these securities allow long-term borrowing through short-term investor commitments. Every 7 to 35 days, securities are auctioned at new reset rates.

The $330 billion market collapsed in 2008 when financial institutions couldn't find buyers and investments became frozen. Values declined substantially, and investors claimed they weren't informed of liquidity risks. Despite pressure from the SEC forcing some buybacks at face value, over $40 billion in pre-crisis auction-rate notes remains outstanding with most auctions failing due to lack of investor interest.
